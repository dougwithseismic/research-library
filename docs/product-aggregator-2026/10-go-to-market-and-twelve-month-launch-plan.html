Go-to-Market and Twelve-Month Launch Plan

The build sequence, experiments, metrics and stop-or-go gates.

Geography
United Kingdom
Research date
2026-09-03
Status
Published research

What is the launch objective?#

The first twelve months should prove one thing:

A buyer with a real, expensive constraint will use the platform's decision process, select a suitable offer and generate an attributable, validated commercial outcome.

The objective is not to maximise indexed pages, imported SKUs, creator sign-ups or pending commission. Those can grow while the underlying product remains useless.

The recommended launch vertical is premium garden and outdoor systems. The initial decision wedge is robot lawn mowers, supported by greenhouse and outdoor-spa acquisition content. Installed awnings and garden rooms enter only after the retailer-checkout path works.

What must be true before development expands?#

The pre-build gate requires:

  • at least five plausible merchants across three relevant product families;
  • at least three programmes with a realistic path to approval and attribution;
  • product-feed samples or a documented manual-data alternative;
  • at least two merchant offers for a meaningful share of launch products;
  • a category expert who can review the decision ontology;
  • a bounded first catalogue of 50–100 products;
  • written definitions for commercial intent, comparison quality and stale data;
  • one agreed north-star metric: approved revenue per high-intent visit.

If the merchant and data gate fails, more content does not solve the business.

Phase 0: Weeks 1–2 — prove supply and evidence#

Merchant work#

  1. Build a list of twenty candidate programmes and direct merchants.
  2. Record category, commission, cookie, PPC rules, feed availability, finance exclusions, validation timing and public contact route.
  3. Apply only to the five to eight programmes required for the test.
  4. Request explicit confirmation of generic paid-search, product-image and comparison-page permissions.
  5. Obtain sample product feeds and API access.

Catalogue work#

  1. Select forty to sixty robot mowers representing navigation, lawn-size and price bands.
  2. Define the first attribute dictionary.
  3. Measure feed completeness and identifier reliability.
  4. Manually verify the twenty most likely products.
  5. Record conflicts and merchant-specific naming.

Buyer work#

Interview at least ten recent or active buyers. Recruit across:

  • small and large lawns;
  • flat and sloped sites;
  • boundary-wire and wireless systems;
  • self-install and dealer-install buyers;
  • successful and regretted purchases.

Interview questions should reconstruct the decision:

  • What triggered the purchase?
  • Which measurements mattered?
  • What disqualified products?
  • Which claims were difficult to compare?
  • Where did the buyer lose confidence?
  • What happened during installation?
  • What would they pay to avoid repeating?

Do not ask whether they “like the idea of a comparison site.”

Phase-0 gate#

Proceed only if:

  • three monetisable merchants remain credible;
  • thirty products can be identified and compared safely;
  • interviews reveal repeated constraints the selector can solve;
  • no programme term blocks the intended public experience.

Phase 1: Weeks 3–6 — build the minimum decision product#

Public product#

  • Category landing page.
  • Constraint-led robot-mower finder.
  • Canonical product pages.
  • Multi-product comparison.
  • Offer table with price, availability, delivery and verification time.
  • Visible recommendation logic.
  • Affiliate disclosure before commercial links.
  • Five foundational buying guides.

User product#

  • Anonymous project state.
  • Optional account creation after a useful result.
  • Saved shortlist.
  • Email capture for price and stock alerts.
  • Feedback on excluded and recommended products.

Operations#

  • Raw feed retention and validation.
  • Product identity review queue.
  • Price anomaly and stale-offer alerts.
  • Tracked outbound redirect.
  • Transaction import and match queue.
  • Commission state and reconciliation.
  • Correction and source history.

Instrumentation#

Track:

landing → finder start → finder completion → shortlist
→ merchant click → transaction → approved transaction → funded commission

Every stage should have a cohort date, category, product, offer and acquisition source where permitted.

Phase 2: Weeks 7–12 — run the first commercial test#

Organic acquisition#

Publish ten pages that are impossible to produce from a merchant feed alone:

  1. Navigation systems explained against real garden conditions.
  2. Lawn-area claims versus practical coverage.
  3. Slopes, narrow passages and separated lawns.
  4. Tree cover and RTK limitations.
  5. Boundary-wire installation cost and repair.
  6. Theft, storage and winter care.
  7. Dealer installation versus self-install.
  8. Total ownership cost over three years.
  9. A measured buyer case study.
  10. A category methodology and corrections page.

Each article should link to the relevant finder state and comparison rather than a generic category grid.

Paid-search validation#

Run only where programme and acquisition terms permit.

  • Start with canonical purchase and constraint phrases.
  • Send traffic to the decision tool, not a thin affiliate page.
  • Exclude brand terms unless expressly permitted.
  • Cap spend by approved-revenue evidence, not pending transactions.
  • Do not scale from outbound click-through.
  • Hold one landing variant stable long enough to observe validation.

The initial paid test should be sized to learn, not to hit a revenue target. Validation delay means the budget needs a hard cash cap.

Creator validation#

Recruit three to five credible category participants:

  • installer or dealer;
  • experienced owner;
  • garden designer;
  • independent reviewer;
  • technical maintenance voice.

Ask each to produce one structured project or decision contribution. Do not recruit a broad creator cohort until one contribution improves engagement or conversion.

Week-12 gate#

Continue if:

  • at least 20% of high-intent entrants start a decision tool;
  • at least 50% of starters complete it;
  • at least 25% of completed users click a merchant offer;
  • product and offer mismatch remains below 2% in weekly manual sampling;
  • tracked transactions can be matched and reconciled;
  • approved revenue per visit is trending toward a plausible acquisition model;
  • at least one non-paid acquisition source produces engaged visits.

These are working thresholds, not industry benchmarks.

Phase 3: Months 4–6 — expand the customer project#

Add one acquisition-rich product family, preferably greenhouses.

Why greenhouses?#

  • greenhouse for sale returns 9,900 average monthly searches.
  • The decision is specification-heavy.
  • The category shares garden measurements, exposure and preparation with the existing project model.
  • It expands traffic without forcing the installed-lead system immediately.

What to build#

  • Greenhouse attribute ontology.
  • Footprint, exposure and glazing finder.
  • Base and installation cost model.
  • Delivery and replacement-part fields.
  • Twenty to forty canonical products initially.
  • Cross-project saved garden profile.
  • Greenhouse versus premium polytunnel decision guide.

Commercial work#

  • Add two or more specialist merchants.
  • Negotiate improved terms using measured intent and conversion.
  • Measure overlap: how many mower users also explore growing spaces?
  • Add accessory bundles only after the primary high-value choice.

Month-6 gate#

  • Two categories produce tracked approved transactions.
  • No single merchant exceeds 70% of approved commission, or a documented mitigation exists.
  • At least 15% of returning users interact with a saved project or alert.
  • Organic entrances grow across original guides rather than templated product pages alone.
  • Feed operations remain sustainable at the expanded catalogue size.

Phase 4: Months 7–9 — add outdoor wellness and creator projects#

Add hot tubs, swim spas and outdoor saunas as a single project family.

Product work#

  • Space, access and service planner.
  • Electrical, base and running-cost model.
  • Product comparison with delivery and installation.
  • Saved complete garden budget.
  • Price and availability alerts.

Editorial work#

  • Real owner operating-cost diaries.
  • Delivery and access case studies.
  • Noise, privacy and winter-use decisions.
  • Maintenance and service comparisons.
  • Honest “do not buy” cases.

Creator work#

Launch creator projects only with participants who can disclose direct experience. A project should include constraints, chosen equipment, alternatives, total budget, failures and last verification date.

Creator compensation should begin on funded commission and use a small cohort. Measure whether creator projects produce higher shortlist, outbound and approved-sale rates than staff editorial.

Phase 5: Months 10–12 — test installed projects#

Add one quote-led lane: made-to-measure awnings.

Supply-side work#

  • Recruit installers with explicit postcode coverage.
  • Define minimum project, wall types, survey capacity and lead time.
  • Agree accepted-lead definition and rejection rules.
  • Sign data-sharing and lead terms.
  • Establish response-time and buyer-contact standards.

Buyer product#

  • Width and projection estimator.
  • Mounting and building-type questions.
  • Operation, sensors, lighting and heating options.
  • Indicative budget with assumptions.
  • Postcode eligibility.
  • Explicit consent before partner handoff.
  • Lead-status visibility where practical.

Commercial test#

Run a fixed number of free or success-priced leads to establish:

  • completion rate;
  • qualification rate;
  • acceptance rate;
  • contact rate;
  • booked-survey rate;
  • time to first response;
  • quoted and sold value;
  • rejection and dispute reasons.

Do not set a national lead price from ten searches for awning quote. Use actual partner outcomes.

What content estate should exist after one year?#

The target is not a fixed article count. A credible estate might contain:

  • four category methodologies;
  • four interactive decision tools;
  • 150–300 canonical products;
  • live offers from five to ten merchants;
  • twenty original project case studies;
  • thirty to fifty decision guides;
  • four cost and ownership calculators;
  • a corrections and evidence policy;
  • creator projects from five to ten specialists;
  • one installed-project lead lane.

Every published surface should connect to a measurable decision step.

Which acquisition channels should be used?#

Search#

  • Commercial category pages.
  • Constraint and compatibility pages.
  • Project and cost guides.
  • Product pages with original evidence.
  • Comparison and alternative pages.

Email#

  • Saved-project reminders.
  • Price and stock changes.
  • Seasonal preparation.
  • Product and merchant changes.
  • New test results.

Creators and experts#

  • Structured project collections.
  • Co-produced field tests.
  • Installer and owner interviews.
  • Measured referral links.

Merchant distribution#

  • Co-branded selectors only where editorial independence remains clear.
  • Merchant-provided samples under disclosed protocols.
  • Direct commercial terms after evidence of incremental demand.

Paid search#

  • Bounded validation.
  • Non-brand terms only unless permission says otherwise.
  • Contribution-based limits.
  • Category-level holdouts where possible.

Public relations and links#

  • Original datasets such as price movements, specification conflicts or operating costs.
  • Seasonal buyer reports.
  • Field-test findings.
  • Expert-reviewed project calculators.

What should the weekly operating review contain?#

Demand#

  • High-intent entrances.
  • Paid and organic query groups.
  • New versus returning users.
  • Category and project mix.

Decision product#

  • Finder starts and completions.
  • Hard-constraint exclusion frequency.
  • Comparison depth.
  • Shortlists and saved projects.
  • User-reported recommendation usefulness.

Commerce#

  • Outbound CTR.
  • Transactions ingested and matched.
  • Pending, approved and declined commission.
  • Approved revenue per visit.
  • Merchant and creator concentration.

Data quality#

  • Feed freshness.
  • Invalid rows.
  • Identity conflicts.
  • Price mismatches.
  • Broken links.
  • Products below publication threshold.

Lead operations#

  • Specifications submitted.
  • Eligible and accepted leads.
  • Rejection reasons.
  • Partner response time.
  • Surveys and outcomes.
  • Disputes.

What should not happen in year one?#

  • No traditional fashion categories.
  • No generic “all products” expansion.
  • No mass AI-generated product descriptions.
  • No native consumer checkout.
  • No creator payout before funded commission.
  • No public user reviews without review-governance controls.
  • No paid placement disguised as editorial order.
  • No national installer expansion before one local or category lead loop works.
  • No merchant SaaS before merchants receive measurable value.
  • No success claim based on pending transactions or imported catalogue size.

What is the final year-one decision?#

At month twelve, expand only if the platform has:

  • repeatable approved revenue from at least two product families;
  • evidence that decision tools outperform ordinary category pages;
  • at least five monetisable merchants with manageable concentration;
  • a catalogue freshness process that scales;
  • a growing organic or direct audience;
  • honest creator contributions that improve outcomes;
  • one lead lane with defined acceptance and economics;
  • positive contribution on the traffic it chooses to buy.

If those conditions are absent, the correct response is not another vertical. It is to identify whether the failure sits in demand, decision utility, supply, attribution or economics and either repair that layer or stop.
