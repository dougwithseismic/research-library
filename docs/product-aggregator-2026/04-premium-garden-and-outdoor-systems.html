Commercial research series / 04

Premium Garden and Outdoor Systems

The recommended first business and its connected product and installation lanes.

Geography
United Kingdom
Research date
2026-09-03
Status
Published research

What is the business?#

The business helps UK homeowners make expensive, connected decisions about the garden as a working part of the home. It is not a gardening-supplies shop and not a catalogue of ornamental products.

Its promise is:

Plan, compare and buy the systems that make a garden easier to maintain and usable for more of the year.

That promise can hold robot lawn mowers, ride-on mowers, greenhouses, pergolas, awnings, hot tubs, swim spas, outdoor saunas, cabins and garden rooms without becoming arbitrary. The buyer is making a capital decision about land, space, comfort and maintenance. Most products need measurements, preparation, installation or ongoing care.

The product aggregator belongs under the promise. A buyer can read a project guide, estimate a budget, enter constraints, compare a shortlist and then buy from a retailer or request an installer introduction.

Who is the target buyer?#

The initial customer is a UK homeowner with discretionary capital, a private garden and a project worth roughly £1,000–£15,000. Several segments fit:

  • busy households buying automation rather than more garden labour;
  • homeowners treating the garden as an additional room;
  • serious growers investing in durable greenhouses and environmental control;
  • home workers adding cabins, offices or studios;
  • wellness buyers adding hot tubs, swim spas, saunas or cold plunges;
  • older or mobility-conscious owners reducing maintenance and increasing year-round usability;
  • design-conscious renovators choosing shade, structures and concealed technology.

The audience definition should exclude bargain gardening, plants, hand tools, decor and fast-moving low-ticket accessories from the central proposition. Those products can appear as contextual add-ons after the high-value decision is solved.

Why are these categories one vertical rather than a bundle of niches?#

They share six decision dimensions:

  1. Space: footprint, access, orientation, gradients and clearances.
  2. Ground and structure: base preparation, drainage, walls, anchoring and loading.
  3. Power and services: electricity, water, connectivity and safe isolation.
  4. Installation: DIY, assisted delivery, specialist fitting or surveyed project.
  5. Maintenance: consumables, winterisation, cleaning, service and replacement parts.
  6. Planning and permanence: visibility, neighbour impact, permitted development and reversibility.

A site can turn those dimensions into shared buyer profiles and project records. The same garden dimensions can inform a mower, greenhouse, pergola and building recommendation. The same postcode can determine delivery, installer coverage, exposure and planning content. The same saved project can contain several products without the brand becoming a generic marketplace.

How should the information architecture work?#

Automation#

  • Robot lawn mowers.
  • Ride-on and zero-turn mowers for large plots.
  • Irrigation control and pumps, added only after validating AOV.
  • Weather, soil and environmental monitoring as supporting products.

Shade and structures#

  • Retractable and electric awnings.
  • Made-to-measure installed awnings.
  • Aluminium and timber pergolas.
  • Canopies, screens, heating and lighting as project components.

Growing spaces#

  • Aluminium and timber greenhouses.
  • Lean-to and wall greenhouses.
  • Premium polytunnels.
  • Staging, irrigation, ventilation and heating as system components.

Outdoor wellness#

  • Hot tubs.
  • Swim spas.
  • Outdoor and barrel saunas.
  • Cold plunges and heat pumps.

Garden buildings#

  • Log cabins.
  • Garden offices and studios.
  • Summerhouses and workshop buildings where the use case stays high-value.
  • Bases, insulation, electrics and installation.

The navigation should also expose project-led routes: “automate lawn care,” “create patio shade,” “build a home growing setup,” “create a home spa” and “add a garden workspace.” Category and project navigation should meet at the same canonical product data.

Which comparison products should launch first?#

Robot-mower finder#

This is the cleanest first data product because the category has documented £1,000-plus AOV, a live affiliate programme and a rich technical decision set. Navimow advertises 5%-plus commission and a 30-day cookie. Navimow programme

The selector should ask for:

  • mapped and mowable lawn area;
  • number of separate zones;
  • steepest slope;
  • narrowest passage;
  • overhead tree cover and open-sky availability;
  • boundary-wire tolerance;
  • obstacles, pets and children's use;
  • mobile coverage and Wi-Fi;
  • theft exposure;
  • desired installation support.

The result should explain each exclusion. “Not suitable because the rear lawn is connected by a 70 cm passage” is more valuable than a generic score.

Greenhouse finder#

This is the largest explicit commercial acquisition category. The finder should use footprint, exposure, glazing, base, door access, growing plan, snow/wind concern, installation preference and budget. It should calculate the complete installed system, not merely frame price.

A greenhouse comparison needs separate fields for:

  • external footprint and internal growing area;
  • eaves and ridge height;
  • glass, toughened glass or polycarbonate;
  • frame section and bracing;
  • door count and threshold;
  • ventilation included and available;
  • base included or required;
  • anchoring method;
  • delivery zone and lead time;
  • installation service;
  • warranty and replacement-pane availability.

Outdoor-spa planner#

This joins hot tubs, swim spas, saunas and cold plunges around a shared space-and-services assessment. Pro-Line Direct reports £2,000–£5,000 average orders, while Steam & Oak operates a 5% programme across several relevant categories. Pro-Line Direct, Steam & Oak

The planner should model:

  • pad and access requirements;
  • electrical supply;
  • water and drainage;
  • running-cost assumptions;
  • noise and neighbour considerations;
  • cover, shelter and privacy;
  • servicing and winter use;
  • delivery route and crane requirement.

Awning estimator and installer handoff#

This should not pretend that a generic feed price equals an installed quote. Aura reports most made-to-measure residential projects at £2,200–£5,500, including survey and installation. Aura price guide

The estimator should collect width, projection, mounting surface, building type, operation, wind sensing, lighting, heating, access and postcode. The platform can show an indicative band, explain its assumptions and offer an installer introduction. Final prices remain with the installer after survey.

Every programme still needs approval, feed access and term verification. A programme page is evidence of availability, not permission to build a business on it.

The project lane needs a different partner map:

  • postcode-level coverage;
  • minimum and maximum project size;
  • survey capacity;
  • installation lead time;
  • accepted wall and site types;
  • response-time commitment;
  • lead rejection reasons;
  • commercial event and payment terms.

What content would create a moat?#

The content should answer decisions merchants tend to answer only inside their own assortment:

  • measured garden case studies with dimensions, budget and outcome;
  • total installed-cost breakdowns;
  • “what will fit?” planning guides;
  • compatibility and preparation checklists;
  • operating-cost calculators with editable assumptions;
  • ownership diaries after one month, one season and one year;
  • repairability, service coverage and replacement-part comparisons;
  • planning, noise, drainage and power explainers reviewed by appropriate experts;
  • interviews with installers and owners that include failure cases;
  • price-history and stock observations with timestamps.

The editorial voice should be specific and comfortable saying no. A south-facing rendered bungalow with limited fixing options needs a different awning answer from a two-storey brick wall. A tree-covered lawn may undermine an RTK-only mower. A swim spa may fail because delivery access, electrical work or ongoing energy is unacceptable. Those exclusions build trust.

What would the launch wedge be?#

The first public release should contain:

  • 40–60 canonical robot mowers from at least three merchants or manufacturers;
  • one constraint-led mower finder;
  • comparison pages for navigation type, lawn size and slope bands;
  • five original technical explainers;
  • five garden-owner or installer interviews;
  • tracked merchant links with consistent sub-IDs;
  • a saved-garden profile and shortlist;
  • an email follow-up for price and availability changes;
  • one greenhouse demand hub built without mass product ingestion;
  • one outdoor-spa project guide to validate expansion interest.

Robot mowers provide the clearest structured-data proof. Greenhouses and hot tubs provide the larger acquisition pools. The launch should therefore test both the decision product and the audience opportunity without building all five departments.

What are the unit-economics gates?#

For a £1,000 product at 5% commission:

gross commission per approved order = £50
break-even approved-sale rate at £1.06 CPC = 2.12%

At a 60% aggregator-to-merchant click rate, the implied merchant approved-sale rate is approximately 3.53%. Returns, attribution loss, validation and operating cost push the required rate higher.

The project funnel needs a separate equation:

maximum raw-lead cost = accepted-lead value × raw-to-accepted rate

If an installer pays £120 per accepted survey and accepts 35% of submitted enquiries, a raw enquiry is worth at most £42 before platform margin and operating cost. Those numbers are scenarios, not observed terms.

What would make this vertical fail?#

  • Merchant feeds lack stable identifiers or useful technical attributes.
  • The site drifts into £20–£200 garden accessories and destroys AOV discipline.
  • Product rankings are simply commission rankings.
  • A single manufacturer supplies most catalogue and revenue.
  • Quote partners reject leads outside narrow service areas.
  • Installation advice is published without sufficient expertise or caveats.
  • Price and stock refreshes lag merchants.
  • Seasonal traffic does not create enough year-round returning use.
  • Generic retailers and Google Shopping solve price discovery while the site adds no decision value.

What remains unknown?#

  • Twelve-month seasonality by scored commercial phrase.
  • Organic SERP difficulty and link requirements.
  • Feed access and completeness for each programme.
  • Merchant conversion, validation and reversal rates.
  • Actual buyer overlap across the five departments.
  • Installer willingness to pay and geographic capacity.
  • The best first category between robot mowers, greenhouses and outdoor spas.
  • Whether saved projects and alerts materially improve conversion over one-session comparison.

These are the first research and product questions to resolve. The commercial search and AOV screen is already strong enough to justify that work.
