How Event Companies Destroy Value

Pollen, Spacious, The Wing and the working-capital, asset and scaling controls their failures expose.

Geography
UK; selected European and US comparators
Research date
2026-09-03
Status
Published research

The danger is not only an empty room#

Social-leisure companies can look attractive on a gross-sales chart while carrying fragile promises underneath:

  • customer money collected before delivery;
  • venue, talent and supplier commitments;
  • refunds and chargebacks;
  • leases or facility minimums;
  • host and contractor dependence;
  • safety and reputation exposure;
  • a calendar that must be filled again next week;
  • membership entitlements that continue after the launch excitement ends.

Three historical cases make the risks concrete. They are not perfect comparables; each comes from a different adjacent category and period.

Pollen: ticket demand did not make the company safe#

Pollen sold destination event experiences and raised more than $200 million—approximately £142 million at the report's reference rate. A detailed Pragmatic Engineer investigation based on documents and interviews with twenty former employees reports that the company entered administration in August 2022 after rapid hiring, unpaid vendors, delayed refunds, missed pension contributions and unpaid staff. It also reports that $3.2 million—approximately £2.27 million—of not-yet-due instalments were charged across roughly 16,000 bookings during the crisis. Investigation

This is secondary investigative reporting rather than an insolvency filing, and the article includes company responses to disputed points. The lesson does not depend on adjudicating every allegation:

Advance customer cash and headline gross bookings can coexist with severe working-capital and governance failure.

Controls for a new operator:

  • separate booked gross value, earned revenue, supplier cash and customer liability;
  • maintain a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast;
  • ring-fence a refund and cancellation reserve;
  • reconcile payment schedules to contractual due dates;
  • show ageing for every venue, speaker, host and contractor payable;
  • give the board or advisers current cash, burn and delivery liability;
  • stop selling formats that cannot be fulfilled or refunded safely.

Spacious: partner value did not survive ownership and cost structure#

Spacious turned restaurants that were empty during the day into paid coworking spaces. WeWork acquired it in August 2019 and shut it by the end of that year. Contemporary reporting described dozens of restaurant and retail locations and membership from a $20 day pass (approximately £14.18) to $129 monthly (approximately £91.49), while the closure was tied to WeWork's retrenchment and questions over Spacious's own expenses relative to revenue. Acquisition, closure

The adjacency is instructive. Using off-peak hospitality capacity can create real partner revenue, but it does not automatically create a durable platform. The operator still needs sufficient member density, favourable space economics and strategic control.

Controls:

  • avoid long guarantees until demand by slot is observed;
  • show venue contribution and company contribution separately;
  • diversify venues and distribution;
  • make contracts survivable if a parent, investor or channel changes strategy;
  • retain direct customer permission and operational data where lawful;
  • do not confuse a delighted partner with a profitable company.

The Wing: identity and a waitlist did not cover fixed assets#

The Wing combined coworking, community and events in designed physical spaces. Fortune reported that it closed its six remaining locations in August 2022 because it had not recovered enough active membership and event activity for a financially sustainable operation. The business had once reached a reported $365 million valuation—approximately £259 million—and a 35,000-person waiting list. Fortune closure report

The pandemic materially affected the case, and the company had governance and cultural controversies beyond this report's scope. The relevant economic lesson is narrower:

Brand heat, identity and waitlists are not a substitute for active paid utilisation against leases and payroll.

Controls:

  • stay venue-light until repeat use, not signups, supports fixed space;
  • measure members by monthly contribution after facility cost;
  • stress-test at 50%, 65% and 80% of expected utilisation;
  • cap annual prepayment if long-run solvency is unproved;
  • distinguish a destination venue strategy from an event-network strategy.

Ten recurring value-destruction patterns#

1. Counting GMV as revenue#

A £35 dinner paid directly to a restaurant is not the social operator's revenue. A £100 experience with a 20% marketplace fee produces £20 platform revenue before refunds and support, not £100.

2. Treating prepayment as free capital#

Credits, annual membership, course deposits and trip instalments create delivery or refund obligations. Cash can arrive before profit and leave before the event.

3. Expanding listings instead of liquidity#

Ten cities with one unreliable night each are not a network. City count, event count and registration count can all rise while successful attended rooms decline.

4. Signing fixed space before proving repeated use#

Pottery, games and members' clubs can justify physical venues. The founder should first prove capacity by time band, corporate demand, access-member behaviour and the full cost of staff, utilities, storage and maintenance.

5. Underpaying the human layer#

Hosts, speakers, chapter leads, coaches and game masters are not interchangeable content. If their effective hourly pay is poor after preparation, travel and follow-up, quality and continuity fail.

6. Selling unlimited access against expensive fulfilment#

Unlimited works when marginal use is cheap or constrained by naturally unused inventory. It fails when every visit consumes a paid instructor, meal, kit, small-group place or prime facility slot.

7. Becoming a bespoke agency accidentally#

Corporate revenue can rescue cash flow while destroying product focus. Every custom brief should be mapped to a standard format, priced for exceptions and reviewed for reusable learning.

8. Letting sponsors choose the culture#

Brand money can subsidise access and accelerate media. If programming becomes an advert, the audience asset that justified sponsorship deteriorates.

9. Automating composition before there is supply#

A matching model cannot create the missing sixth player, twelfth dinner guest or second wheelchair-accessible venue. Human exception handling and honest cancellation remain necessary.

10. Calling attendance wellbeing impact#

An event can be enjoyable without clinically reducing loneliness or improving mental health. Institutional and public-sector sales must distinguish participation, self-reported experience and validated outcomes.

A working-capital dashboard#

The board or founder should see this weekly:

Measure Why it matters
unrestricted cash money actually available
customer cash collected gross inflow
undelivered ticket and credit value future service or refund exposure
committed venue and talent payments supplier obligation
aged refunds and chargebacks trust and solvency warning
event contribution by delivery month profitability of what has actually happened
next eight weeks' minimum guarantees downside if sales stop
member entitlement utilisation bands variable-cost tail hidden by the average
host and facilitator payables whether labour is being financed by delayed payment
sales concentration exposure to one format, sponsor, venue or city

Stop conditions#

Pause a format, city or revenue line when:

  • refunds cannot be paid from available reserves;
  • supplier arrears are used to make contribution look positive;
  • full-price fill remains below the go/no-go threshold for two cycles;
  • founder labour is the only source of apparent margin;
  • a membership cohort has negative contribution at ordinary utilisation;
  • one host, venue, speaker or sponsor accounts for more than 30% of delivered value without a replacement plan;
  • safety cases cannot be handled within the published service level;
  • a second city needs materially higher subsidy without an identified temporary cause;
  • paid promotion causes organisers to doubt ranking integrity;
  • B2B custom work consumes capacity required for the repeat consumer product.

These are management gates, not industry benchmarks.

The ultimate pitfall#

The category is seductive because the experience is visible. A full bar, a smiling group and a beautiful app look like product-market fit. The durable business sits underneath the photograph:

earned revenue
− fully allocated direct delivery
− refunds and future obligations
− repeat acquisition cost
− paid human labour
= value that can fund the next room

If that equation is not visible, more cities, more formats and more capital make the company less knowable rather than more valuable.
