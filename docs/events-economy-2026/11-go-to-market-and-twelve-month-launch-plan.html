Go-to-Market and Twelve-Month Launch Plan

A staged UK launch with experiments, metrics, expansion rules and stop conditions.

Geography
UK; selected European and US comparators
Research date
2026-09-03
Status
Internal working research

The objective#

The first year should prove one repeatable regional cell, not maximise cities, registrations or press mentions.

The core question is:

Can the company repeatedly fill hosted, platonic small-group events for one life-stage segment, create positive event contribution and convert first attendance into a second booking?

Months 0–1: discovery before inventory#

Interview at least:

  • 12 prospective attendees across new-to-area, remote-work, post-separation, retirement and existing-local segments;
  • 6 restaurant operators covering quiet weekday and busy weekend venues;
  • 6 experienced hosts or community organisers;
  • 3 safeguarding, event-risk or customer-operations specialists.

Ask for recent behaviour, not agreement with the concept. “When did you last try to meet new people?” is useful. “Would you use an app that helps you meet people?” is not.

Map a service area by realistic evening travel time. Select two venue price bands and two recurring weekday slots. Do not announce the city until there are viable venue and host backups.

Gate#

Proceed only if at least 20 target participants provide a real availability window and 12 accept a stated £12–£18 reservation test. Interest without time and price is not demand.

Months 2–3: concierge pilot#

Run four to six paid tables using existing payment and event tooling. Use manual group assignment with documented reasons. The founder should observe but not host every event; otherwise host portability remains untested.

For each event capture:

  • inquiries, checkout starts and paid bookings;
  • booking lead time;
  • cancellations before and after the boundary;
  • predicted and actual attendance;
  • host and venue time;
  • direct cost and contribution;
  • arrival comfort and severe-comfort reports;
  • rebooking within 30 and 60 days;
  • source and referral.

Gate#

Do not build membership if fewer than three events clear minimum size or if first attendees do not actively request a next occasion.

Months 4–5: repeat the same promise#

Run the same two slots and formats rather than adding novelty. Recruit a second and backup host. Establish a venue scorecard and negotiate a repeat operating agreement based on actual group behaviour.

Launch a simple referral:

referrer receives credit only after the new guest attends
new guest receives a modest first-event credit
both rewards are capped and recorded separately from purchased credits

Do not reward registrations. The useful outcome is a new attended guest.

Gate#

Aim for at least 85% show-up and 25% first-to-second booking within 60 days. These are management thresholds, not external benchmarks. If repeat is weak, interview attendees and non-returners before changing price or adding formats.

Months 6–7: productise operations#

Build only the workflow manual operations have exposed:

  • member and consent record;
  • event capacity and thresholds;
  • booking and credits ledger;
  • group assignment with review and reason codes;
  • waitlist and reconfirmation;
  • host run sheet and incident route;
  • attendance and contribution reporting;
  • data-subject request and deletion workflow.

Move operational truth out of personal spreadsheets and chat threads. Do not add public profiles, direct messaging or AI personality matching.

Gate#

Every booking, credit, assignment, attendance and safety action should be reconcilable. If the team cannot explain a group or cash balance, adding another event increases risk.

Months 8–9: test the habit#

Offer a capped membership to the most active cohorts. A reasonable experiment is £24–£32 monthly for up to two standard reservations, with early access and paid additional seats. Keep pay-as-you-go available so the test does not force conversion.

Measure:

  • invitation-to-trial rate;
  • attended seats per member-month;
  • direct cost per member-month;
  • membership contribution;
  • one-, two- and three-month retention;
  • whether members crowd out pay-as-you-go customers;
  • unused entitlement and stated value;
  • effect on cancellations and no-shows.

Gate#

Do not offer unlimited access unless the upper-utilisation cohort remains contribution-positive and inventory availability is consistently good.

Months 10–12: one adjacency#

Choose one adjacency based on member evidence:

  • a weekend walk and coffee;
  • an alcohol-light supper;
  • a daytime table for retired or flexible members;
  • a newcomer series with an employer or residential partner;
  • a neighbouring town inside the same operational radius.

Run it as a separate cohort P&L. Do not bundle weak events into membership to hide demand.

Final gate#

Expand only if the original cell has positive direct contribution, repeat attendance, host redundancy, venue redundancy and a credible incident record. Otherwise remain local, raise price, change the cohort or stop.

Acquisition portfolio#

1. Referrals#

The best channel because social trust transfers between people. Reward attendance, not signups. Track referrer quality and prevent one promoter from distorting a cohort.

2. Venue distribution#

Use table cards, bill inserts, newsletters and staff recommendations only where the venue can explain the product accurately. Give each venue a trackable but privacy-preserving source code.

3. Local editorial#

Local radio, newspapers, neighbourhood newsletters and founder stories fit a regional trust service. The pitch should be the actual experience and audience, not unsupported loneliness outcomes.

4. Search#

Build landing pages around specific intent—meeting people locally, friendship events and hosted dinners. Use paid search as a bounded experiment because the CPC-to-first-seat economics are demanding.

5. Partnerships#

Test relocation providers, coworking spaces, alumni groups, libraries, cultural venues and large employers. Keep institutional cohorts separate until members explicitly choose cross-cohort events.

6. Social content#

Show the arrival, host and venue without exposing attendees who did not give specific media consent. Testimonials should identify when an attendee received free access or another benefit.

The metric tree#

qualified local reach
→ event-page view
→ paid booking
→ reconfirmed booking
→ attended seat
→ comfortable event
→ second booking
→ referred attended guest
→ 180-day contribution

Supporting operational measures:

  • event fill at 14 days, 7 days and 72 hours;
  • show-up rate;
  • waitlist recovery;
  • host and venue cancellation;
  • contribution per occupied seat;
  • direct support time per event;
  • credits outstanding;
  • material incident and resolution rate;
  • cohort concentration by acquisition source.

Experiments worth running#

Question Test Primary measure Decision
Does hosting matter? Same format with trained host versus light-touch coordinator Arrival comfort and second booking Keep the service level that produces incremental repeat
Does age guidance help? Broad adult table versus life-stage-guided table Fill, comfort and repeat Use the least restrictive segmentation that works
Does commitment reduce no-shows? Refundable deposit versus £15 reservation Attended contribution Prefer the simplest effective commitment
Are credits useful? Direct ticket versus three-seat pack Cash, usage and repeat Keep only if breakage and liability are controlled
Which slot is viable? Tuesday versus Thursday Fill and contribution Concentrate inventory rather than maintain weak choice
Can venues acquire members? Tracked venue referral Cost per attended first guest Share value only after attendance

What not to do in year one#

  • Launch ten cities from a waitlist map.
  • Promise algorithmic compatibility before observing group quality.
  • Mix dating and friendship without separate intent and events.
  • Build direct messaging before moderation capacity exists.
  • Subsidise empty rooms to produce photos.
  • Use free events as evidence for paid retention.
  • report restaurant spend as platform GMV without explaining the split.
  • sell brand access to sensitive member segments.
  • expand because a city achieved registrations rather than repeat attendance.

The first year's output is not a national brand. It is a verified operating cell and an honest answer about whether it should be copied.
