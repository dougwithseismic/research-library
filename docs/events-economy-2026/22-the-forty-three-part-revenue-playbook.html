The Forty-Three-Part Revenue Playbook

Consumer, content, B2B, venue, brand and platform revenue sequenced by the asset required.

Geography
UK; selected European and US comparators
Research date
2026-09-03
Status
Published research

Revenue is a sequence, not a menu#

The revenue playbook records 43 distinct mechanisms observed across the company set or explicitly proposed for later testing. A founder should not launch 43 revenue lines. The point is to see which assets can support which revenue at each stage.

The governing rule is:

Add a revenue layer only when the product has already created the scarce asset that makes the buyer rational.

A ticket can exist when one event exists. An access membership needs scarcity. A venue fee needs proven incremental covers. Sponsorship needs a known audience. Content licensing needs rights. SaaS needs a workflow worth paying for. A marketplace commission needs demand the host could not easily obtain alone.

The seven economic assets#

Asset created Revenue it can support
A desirable individual event ticket, VIP, two-person bundle, private booking
Repeated consumer behaviour pack, capped membership, course, cohort programme
Scarcity and identity access-only membership, patron tier, merchandise
Reusable programming or rights digital ticket, recording, CPD, podcast, content licence
Incremental venue demand per-cover fee, preferred-partner fee, minimum or revenue share
A defined reachable audience sponsorship, sampling, paid placement, corporate and group sales
Supply workflow and transactions organiser SaaS, processing, discovery commission, chapter licence

Trying to sell the revenue before creating the asset produces fake diversification: five weak lines attached to the same unproven event.

Stage one: revenue that proves the room#

Single-event ticket#

This is the cleanest demand test. Publish one price, one capacity and one refund boundary. Recognise revenue against delivery rather than treating every advance booking as earned cash.

The ticket should be chosen to test the real proposition. A £2 deposit mainly tests whether people will surrender £2. Heavy discounts can fill a room while teaching nothing about sustainable acquisition.

Two-person bundle#

Seed Talks uses two-person ticket options. The bundle reduces the psychological cost of arriving alone and can lower acquisition cost per attendee. It also risks making solo visitors feel peripheral. The event design should state explicitly that solo attendance is normal.

VIP or priority#

Reserved seating, early access and priority questions add yield without doubling delivery cost. They work best where demand exceeds capacity. They are weak when sold as artificial status in a half-empty room.

Deposit and no-show economics#

Late-cancel rules protect perishable seats. They should be understandable, proportionate and accompanied by a waitlist. The economic objective is reliable attendance, not fee extraction.

Stage two: make return behaviour purchasable#

Packs and credits#

Credits smooth repeat checkout and create cash earlier. They also create a future service obligation. The operator needs a subledger for issued, purchased, promotional, redeemed, expired, transferred and refunded credit.

Ocio's three current plans show that credits can span heterogeneous experiences. Dinners With Friends uses credits around one core format. The first is a portfolio product; the second is a repeat-payment mechanism. Their redemption and margin risks differ.

Capped membership#

For a young operator, the safest membership includes a bounded number of standard bookings plus early access, free transfers or member pricing. It makes monthly value clear without giving the heaviest users an unlimited claim on delivery.

Unlimited membership#

Timeleft and Reading Rhythms show the attraction. Unlimited access is a powerful habit promise when the city has frequent inventory and the marginal event place is cheap. It is dangerous where every use consumes a host, material, meal subsidy or facility slot.

The operator must model contribution by member utilisation band, not only the average:

non-user
light user
median user
heavy user
extreme user

An average can hide a small cohort that consumes most capacity.

Access-only membership#

Lectures on Tap's $49.99 membership is strategically important because tickets remain separate. The member buys priority and belonging rather than event consumption. This can have excellent gross margin, but only if the event is genuinely scarce and the perks remain meaningful.

Cohort programme#

Adventure Solos charges £195 monthly for twelve months. Courses, seasons, leagues and TTRPG campaigns are milder versions of the same mechanism. The customer commits to repeated exposure and progress; the operator receives predictable demand.

The liability must be explicit. Ten members on a £2,340 programme generate £23,400 contracted revenue and a year of promised operations. That is not equivalent to £23,400 of delivered profit.

Stage three: increase revenue per participant#

Objects, materials and access#

Pottery Cafe separates a studio fee from the object. Social Pottery sells courses and studio access. This creates a ladder from occasion to practice. The operator must allocate material, firing, breakage, storage and instructor cost to the correct product.

Premium formats#

DEIGHT's live calendar makes the ladder visible: regular £20 events included for members, £39 brunches at £29 for members, and premium formats up to £95 with a member discount. DEIGHT

Premium should mean a materially different cost or experience, not the same room with a new adjective.

Retreats and trips#

High order values can fund deeper experience and relationships. They also introduce accommodation, travel, safeguarding, vendor, cancellation and working-capital risk. Customer cash should be ring-fenced operationally against delivery and refund exposure.

Gifts#

Gift cards introduce another buyer and work well for classes, talks and experiences. The recipient may have different availability and interests from the purchaser, so expiry, transfer and redemption design matters.

Stage four: reuse the programme#

Live digital and on-demand#

Seed can sell the same intellectual property into a venue, live stream and twelve-month recording. 5x15 and Intelligence Squared maintain substantial audio or video catalogues. The marginal delivery cost can be low after capture, but audience acquisition and completion remain real.

The rights agreement should settle:

  • live streaming;
  • full recording sales;
  • clips and paid social use;
  • transcript and course derivatives;
  • geographic and time limits;
  • speaker approval and revenue share;
  • third-party images, music and confidential material.

CPD and professional education#

Seed's CPD add-on and professional workshops increase willingness to pay for appropriate subjects. The operator must not imply accreditation or professional competence beyond what the issuing process supports.

Media and sponsorship#

A stage can create a podcast; a chapter network can create local media inventory; a visual ritual can create sponsor content. The media audience and the event audience should be measured separately. A million video views do not prove a hundred paid seats in Bristol.

Stage five: sell to organisations#

Corporate team events#

Social Pottery, Brush Party and Fever show the range from standard workshop to full-service production. Corporate sessions can fill weekdays, bring higher order values and use the same facilitator system.

The margin disappears when every client receives a new format, deck, venue search, run sheet and reporting package. Offer a small menu with priced options and a clear change-order rule.

Employee benefit#

Urban Sports Club, Wellhub and Fever Pass aggregate activity into an HR product. The value is breadth, eligibility, invoicing and use across a workforce. It is plausible only once the operator can fulfil beyond one weekly format.

Venue and property buyer#

Venues may pay for off-peak covers or programming. Property owners may fund activity that increases footfall or makes a district feel alive. Reading Rhythms markets destination activations, and Fever sells hotel and property experiences.

The operator needs a counterfactual. Footfall is not incremental if the same people would have visited anyway. Report attended people, spend where consented, repeat visits and a credible baseline.

Brand buyer#

Timeleft, Reading Rhythms, Daybreaker and Fever package real audiences for brands. This can become high-margin revenue and subsidise access. It can also corrupt curation. The sponsor must fit the member's reason for attending, and paid involvement must be obvious.

Stage six: monetise the network#

Organiser software#

Liiiv, Playtomic and Meetup charge or plan around supply-side tools. Internal software becomes sellable only when it repeatedly saves time or raises revenue for third parties. A founder should resist splitting attention into SaaS because an operations dashboard looks polished.

Transaction and discovery fees#

The public market spans Eventbrite's ticket fee, StartPlaying's 15%, Airbnb Experiences' typical 20%, SweatPals' separate 20% discovery commission and Eatwith's 30% guest-side commission. The higher the take, the more demand, insurance, support or operating value the platform must deliver.

Chapter or city licence#

Reading Rhythms pays local leads from tickets; Pint of View reportedly licenses its brand to independent chapters. This transfers local labour and some risk. It also makes underpaid, inconsistent or inactive chapters a central-brand problem.

Publish a chapter P&L before celebrating capital-light expansion.

Illustrative format economics#

The report now includes twelve transparent format scenarios. Selected cases are:

Format Gross revenue Direct contribution Direct margin
Hosted ten-seat dinner £150 £88 58.7%
100-person public lecture £2,000 £610 30.5%
150-person public lecture £3,750 £1,650 44.0%
40-person reading party £600 £218 36.3%
36-person social game night £540 £164 30.4%
20-person pickup sport £240 £56 23.3%
12-person portable pottery £540 £97 18.0%

These are decision cases, not benchmarks. Only the dinner seat and host contribution have direct operator anchors; other prices use observed market examples but all combined cost stacks are assumptions. The dinner's apparent margin excludes the diner meal, host free-ticket treatment and central overhead. It should not be read as the easiest or most profitable format.

The recommended revenue stack by phase#

Months 0–3#

  • standard ticket;
  • season or course pass;
  • two-person option;
  • corporate version quoted from the same format;
  • no membership.

Months 4–12#

  • ticket and three-event pack;
  • capped membership only after repeat proof;
  • one premium small-group format;
  • gift card;
  • repeat corporate package;
  • venue benefit measured but not necessarily charged.

Months 12–24#

  • access or capped membership by format economics;
  • touring or chapter version of a proven programme;
  • recordings and CPD where rights and subject support them;
  • brand pilot with transparent reporting;
  • lightweight partner inventory.

Months 24–36#

  • employer or property programme;
  • marketplace or credit pass across trusted supply;
  • organiser tools where third parties request them;
  • city licence only after two company-run cells work;
  • a deliberate choice between cash-generative operator and funded network.

Revenue quality hierarchy#

For this company, revenue quality should be judged in this order:

  1. delivered positive contribution;
  2. repeat purchase without fresh paid acquisition;
  3. recurring revenue with bounded fulfilment;
  4. prepaid revenue with a reconciled liability;
  5. B2B revenue that reuses the standard format;
  6. sponsorship that preserves member trust;
  7. marketplace or software revenue backed by real demand;
  8. speculative revenue from a future audience.

The best revenue is not the one with the most software-like label. It is the one that survives honest allocation of delivery, acquisition, refunds, founder labour and future obligations.
