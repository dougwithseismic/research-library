# Discovery platforms: Liiiv, Ocio and Fever

## What is a local discovery platform?

A local discovery platform helps a person turn an open block of time into a real booking or visit across third-party events, venues and activities. It is not primarily the event producer. Its product is a faster, more confident decision from fragmented local supply.

The category spans several different businesses that are often collapsed into “things to do”:

- a media guide earns advertising or sponsorship from attention;
- a listing marketplace earns commission when a ticket transacts;
- a credit membership buys or negotiates access and resells it inside a monthly allowance;
- an organiser operating system earns software and processing fees before adding discovery;
- an original-experience platform uses discovery data to produce its own higher-margin formats.

The consumer job is occasional but urgent: _what should I do tonight, with these people, at this price, in this part of the city?_ The supply-side job is continuous: keep inventory current, prove incremental demand, attribute bookings and settle money. That asymmetry is why a beautiful feed can have abundant listings but weak habit and weak revenue.

The core unit is not an app download or an event page view. It is a completed, attributable participation that improves the next decision and gives the organiser a reason to remain in the system.

## Liiiv is a different company from Timeleft

Timeleft assembles people into operator-designed events. Liiiv helps a person decide which third-party event, venue or creator fits their evening. Conflating them produces the wrong product, capital plan and metrics.

Liiiv's current product loop is:

```text
taste and location
→ personalised swipeable event and place feed
→ save or share
→ external reservation or in-app ticket
→ learn preference from behaviour
```

Its site includes concerts, brunches, restaurants, workshops, exhibitions and free events. It currently operates as a single-city European product and is free to consumers. It is included here to expose a discovery model, not as evidence of UK demand. [Liiiv](https://www.liiiv.cz/cs)

The Android store listed 10,000-plus downloads on 3 September 2026. This is a distribution signal, not proof of retained usage. The listing also says users can book tickets or reservations and that recommendations improve with use. [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?hl=cs&id=com.creativedock.liiiv)

## The public terms reveal the model

Liiiv's organiser terms are unusually informative. They describe:

- a **free promotional model** for no-ticket events;
- a **provider-ticket model** that redirects to an organiser's external checkout;
- a **Tickets by Liiiv model** with Stripe Connect, electronic tickets and settlement;
- a free organiser tier and paid **PRO subscription**;
- commission set by a price list inside the organiser interface;
- paid commercial promotion that can influence ranking;
- relevance, location, popularity, preferences and search as other ranking inputs;
- payouts normally three to seven business days after the event;
- organiser responsibility for the event, content and many customer claims.

[Liiiv B2B terms](https://www.liiiv.cz/b2b-terms-of-service)

That creates at least four possible revenue lines:

| Revenue line                | What Liiiv must prove                     | Main risk                                  |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| PRO organiser subscription  | workflow or analytics is worth paying for | free tier is sufficient                    |
| Ticket commission           | enough paid inventory transacts in-app    | external redirects keep the economics thin |
| Paid promotion              | Liiiv supplies measurable attention       | sponsored ranking damages trust            |
| Referral or future commerce | a booking can be attributed and paid      | loss of customer and settlement data       |

The exact public pricing and take rate were not visible. No revenue estimate should be constructed from downloads alone.

## Liiiv's hardest problem is frequency, not breadth

A city-discovery app can ingest thousands of events and still be a weak habit. The customer only needs it when plans are unresolved. Google Maps, Instagram, TikTok, venue newsletters, friends, ticketing platforms and search all compete in that moment.

The defensible loop would be:

```text
high-confidence local inventory
→ fast decision
→ completed booking or visit
→ trustworthy preference signal
→ better next recommendation
→ direct return before the next open evening
```

Likes and swipes are weaker than completed attendance. A founder should measure:

- weekly users with at least one genuinely available leisure window;
- shortlist-to-detail and detail-to-booking conversion;
- external redirect completion where it can be consented and attributed;
- repeat active use before another event need;
- saved-to-expired inventory ratio;
- booking cancellation and event cancellation;
- organiser renewal and revenue per active organiser;
- concentration of bookings by venue, category and source;
- percentage of inventory that is stale, duplicated or unavailable.

## Liiiv, Ocio and Fever are three different versions of discovery

### Liiiv: free consumer utility, organiser monetisation

The user browses broadly. Organisers pay for tools, transaction and attention. This can be capital-efficient if supply onboarding is light and local media or venue relationships drive distribution. It also risks becoming an attractive guide with weak monetisation.

### Ocio: paid consumer concierge with credits

Ocio currently charges £42, £67 or £92 per month for 38, 60 or 83 credits, respectively. It curates London after-work activities across sixteen neighbourhoods and lets a member book up to six tickets. [Ocio](https://ocio.events/)

Ocio owns more of the consumer wallet and therefore more inventory-cost risk. The key metric is not membership revenue alone:

```text
member revenue
− partner redemption cost
− refunds and support
− unused-credit liability movement
= contribution per member-month
```

The almost constant £1.11 price per credit means higher plans do not currently create a strong nominal discount. They may still improve commitment and absolute gross profit. Without partner payout terms, the credit has no observable economic value.

### Fever: media, marketplace and owned formats

Fever combines an experience marketplace with original formats and a media network. Its current business site sells private corporate events, group bookings, an employee Fever Pass, gift cards, brand sponsorship, custom activation and advertising. It claims hundreds of cities and a large experience catalogue, but the figures vary across product pages and are first-party. [Corporate products](https://business.feverup.com/en/corporate-services/), [brand products](https://business.feverup.com/brand-partnerships/)

Fever's deeper model is not a ticket directory. Distribution identifies demand; original formats capture more economics; media lowers acquisition; B2B sells the catalogue to another payer.

## Vertical platforms have an easier starting point

Generic discovery asks an organiser to maintain another listing and a user to open another feed. Vertical systems can solve an operational problem before the network is large.

- **Playtomic** manages a club's schedule, payments, classes, courses, leagues, coaches and memberships while exposing inventory to players. [Playtomic](https://playtomic.com/pricing)
- **SweatPals** gives a host ticketing, waivers, messaging, memberships and packs, then charges an additional discovery commission where it supplies the attendee. [SweatPals](https://www.sweatpals.com/lp)
- **StartPlaying** gives professional game masters booking, discovery, payment and support, taking 15% of bookings. [StartPlaying](https://intercom.help/startplaying/en/articles/14724019-what-does-the-15-fee-cover)
- **ClassPass** helps venues release excess capacity into a consumer credit network while setting confidential payout floors. [ClassPass](https://classpass.com/partners/blog/classpass-payouts-pricing-policies-rates)

The repeated pattern is:

```text
solve supply workflow
→ see live capacity
→ route demand into it
→ settle payment
→ learn actual utilisation
```

That is a more robust cold-start strategy than collecting a city calendar and hoping organisers and consumers arrive simultaneously.

## The marketplace margin ladder

Observed platform charges illustrate what different kinds of value can support:

| Platform           | Publicly observed mechanism                                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Eventbrite UK      | 6.95% + £0.59 service fee; optional organiser fee                   |
| StartPlaying       | 15% of game-master bookings plus a small player checkout fee        |
| Airbnb Experiences | typically 20% deducted from host payout                             |
| SweatPals          | 3% or 7% service fee, processing and 20% discovery commission       |
| Eatwith            | 30% commission added to the host price                              |
| Liiiv              | undisclosed in-interface commission plus optional PRO and promotion |
| ClassPass          | confidential partner payout floor inside a credit subscription      |

[Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.co.uk/help/en-ca/articles/755615/how-much-does-it-cost-for-organizers-to-use-eventbrite/), [Airbnb](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3164), [Eatwith](https://intercom.help/eatwith/en/articles/432396-does-eatwith-charge-a-commission-on-the-price-i-pay-for-my-experience)

These percentages are not directly comparable. Some include demand generation, insurance or support; some sit on top of consumer price; some come out of the supplier payout; some apply only to discovered bookings. The important question is what incremental function justifies the take.

## The bootstrap trap: building Liiiv before owning a reason to open it

A multi-category city app appears lighter than running events. In practice it needs:

- broad, current and deduplicated inventory;
- organiser onboarding and support;
- ranking and commercial-disclosure rules;
- attribution across external systems;
- payments, KYC, refunds and chargebacks for internal tickets;
- consumer acquisition in competition with established city guides;
- enough repeated use to sell promotion or subscription credibly;
- a clear boundary when a third-party event fails.

This is fundable platform work, but a difficult bootstrap starting point. The founder can spend a year building supply without creating a habit or meaningful transaction volume.

## The stronger bootstrap sequence

Own one flagship event format first:

```text
1. create a distinctive recurring night
2. build a direct audience and attendance history
3. cross-sell a few trusted partner events
4. introduce a paid local pass or membership
5. give partners lightweight inventory and settlement tools
6. expand discovery only where the audience already asks for breadth
```

This sequence makes the platform emerge from owned demand. The founder can still use Liiiv, Eventbrite or Fever as distribution rather than rebuilding their functionality. If the owned format cannot create a returning audience, a broader app is unlikely to repair the problem.

## When a funded discovery route makes sense

Venture or strategic capital may be appropriate if the company can establish at least one of these before a large consumer build:

- exclusive or technically integrated local inventory;
- a media audience with measurable event conversion;
- an employer or university distribution contract;
- a venue operating system that creates real-time capacity;
- an owned original format with strong repeat;
- transaction density in one city and category;
- a cost advantage in recommendation, settlement or organiser acquisition.

The pitch is then not “there is lots happening”. It is “we control the signal that turns abundant activity into completed participation, and the unit remains valuable in every new city.”

## The strategic verdict on Liiiv

Liiiv validates the broad category and exposes a real local problem: a city can have too much fragmented supply for a person to choose confidently. It also demonstrates several monetisation routes on the organiser side.

It does not remove the need for an owned wedge. For the founder considered in this report, Liiiv should be treated first as a potential distribution partner, local inventory source or comparator. Only after the company has an audience and proprietary participation data should it choose whether to become a discovery layer itself.
