# Forty-four-company operator atlas

## What the operator census contains

The [operator census](../evidence/operator-census.csv) covers 44 active or strategically relevant companies and products across managed friendship, dating events, talks, reading, games, craft, sport, discovery, marketplaces and creator infrastructure.

This is a commercial atlas, not a claim that every operator is a direct competitor. Each row records its customer job, payer, observed revenue layers, evidence class, source IDs, useful strategic read and the most important unknown. A live page proves that an offer exists; it does not prove profitability, retention or service quality.

## Twelve operators worth studying closely

### 1. Lectures on Tap: content as nightlife

Its sequence—twenty minutes to arrive and socialise, an interactive opener, a 45-minute expert lecture, Q&A and post-talk conversation—is deliberately more social than a theatre seat and more substantive than a mixer. It sells tickets through Eventbrite and a $49.99 monthly access membership that explicitly excludes tickets. [Format](https://lecturesontap.com/), [membership](https://lecturesontap.com/members)

The important invention is not “professor in bar”. It is making intellectual identity a reason to go out.

### 2. Seed Talks: one programme, many revenue surfaces

Seed runs live talks across a long list of UK cities and also sells online talks, recordings, multi-week courses, summits, professional workshops and CPD. It uses VIP seating, two-person offers, gift cards and a limited pay-what-you-can allocation. Its homepage claims more than 1,000 talks, 200,000 guests and a 100,000-person mailing list. Those are first-party figures. [Seed Talks](https://www.seedtalks.co.uk/), [talks in pubs](https://www.seedtalks.co.uk/talks-in-pubs)

This is the strongest evidence in the census that programming can become an IP and audience business rather than a sequence of isolated rooms.

### 3. Reading Rhythms: ritual before subject expertise

The product does not need a famous author or a common book. People bring their own book, read together and then talk. Chapter leads receive a playbook, earn a percentage of ticket sales and typically host one or two events a month. The company sells tiered Passport memberships and brand products spanning chapter sponsorship, large events, product sampling, newsletters, UGC and content licensing. [Chapter model](https://readingrhythms.co/apply/), [partnerships](https://readingrhythms.co/partnerships/), [Passport](https://readingrhythms.co/passport/)

It shows that a repeatable social protocol can be more scalable than a large content-production burden.

### 4. Social Pottery: the activity stack

Social Pottery combines one-off painting and making, six-week courses, studio access, memberships, gifts, parties, mobile delivery and corporate workshops across five studios. Its Camden offer ranges from £15 hourly drop-in to £110 and £220 monthly access plans. [Social Pottery](https://socialpottery.com/), [Camden memberships](https://socialpottery.com/locations/camden-pottery-making/)

This is commercially dense. One customer can move from first date to course to regular maker to corporate buyer. The tradeoff is rent, equipment, safety, inventory and firing.

### 5. GoodRec: solve the missing-player problem

GoodRec lets a person choose a city, sport and hosted pickup game, then show up without organising a team. It claims one million players and 100,000-plus games across the US and Canada. [GoodRec](https://www.goodrec.com/)

Sport has an unusually concrete liquidity problem: a match may require exactly the right number of people, level, facility and time. Solving it creates repeat behaviour and a stronger operational moat than a generic event feed.

### 6. Playtomic: marketplace plus operating system

Playtomic sells club software for schedules, payments, public classes, tournaments, leagues, coaches, courses and memberships while connecting players to clubs and open matches. Its pricing page claims more than 6,700 clubs. [Playtomic pricing](https://playtomic.com/pricing)

The model matters because software improves supply before the marketplace demands a take. It is the vertical version of “tools, then network”.

### 7. SweatPals: community entrepreneurs as supply

SweatPals gives fitness hosts tickets, check-in, memberships, class packs, messaging, waivers, analytics and discovery. Its current page lists no monthly subscription, a 7% Starter or 3% Pro service fee, $0.99 plus 2.9% processing and 20% discovery commission. [SweatPals](https://www.sweatpals.com/lp)

Separating the ordinary service fee from the higher discovery commission is an elegant alignment device: the platform earns more when it supplies the customer.

### 8. Adventure Solos: high-ticket friendship as a programme

The Manchester Friendship Builder sells a twelve-month local cohort for £195 a month, including nine or ten outdoor events, a dedicated WhatsApp group and a limited guarantee. [Adventure Solos](https://www.adventuresolos.com/friendship-builder-manchester)

At £2,340 contracted revenue per member, the company can afford a different level of delivery and sales. It also assumes a substantial future-event and refund obligation. This is closer to an outcome programme than an event club.

### 9. DEIGHT: membership made visible through included events

DEIGHT charges £29.99 monthly in Nottingham and Birmingham. Current listings show regular £20 event passes included for members, brunches at £39 versus £29, a £95 versus £75 premium dinner and a £59 versus £49 tasting. It also requires a one-time identity check. [DEIGHT](https://www.deight.events/)

The offer makes monthly value legible: one included regular social can nearly justify the subscription. The unknown is whether utilisation and event delivery leave positive contribution.

### 10. Ocio: credits across the 5–9

Ocio bundles curated London activity inventory into 38 credits for £42, 60 for £67 or 83 for £92 a month. Credits roll over and one member can book up to six people. It spans music, food, culture, classes, wellness, sport, theatre and social events. [Ocio](https://ocio.events/)

The price per credit is almost constant—about £1.11—so the tiers currently create commitment and capacity rather than a meaningful volume discount. Its decisive unknown is the difference between what a credit costs Ocio and what the member pays.

### 11. GO Mammoth: sell a season, not an isolated fixture

GO Mammoth runs organised UK adult leagues across more than ten team sports. Players prepay for a season that normally lasts eight to twelve weeks and can enter alone, in a small group or as a team. A live London basketball listing showed £12.20 per week for an individual and an estimated £7 per week for a player joining as part of a ten-person team. [GO Mammoth](https://www.gomammoth.co.uk/about-us/), [live league example](https://www.gomammoth.co.uk/basketball/bermondsey-southwark/?leagueid=2629)

The commercial asset is the completed recurring fixture: venue, officials, equipment, schedule, standings and enough players at the right level. The season creates commitment and repeated exposure before the operator asks for a membership.

### 12. ClassPass: perishable capacity as the economic substrate

ClassPass tells partners it may pay less than a direct visit because it is intended to sell capacity that would otherwise expire. It agrees a confidential floor, adjusts member credits dynamically and in most cases compensates eligible late cancellations. Its FAQ says it has paid $3.1 billion to partners, a first-party cumulative claim. [Partner payouts](https://classpass.com/partners/blog/classpass-payouts-pricing-policies-rates), [partner FAQ](https://classpass.com/partners/faqs)

The lesson is not to copy its scale. It is to identify which social-leisure capacity is perishable and can be opened without displacing direct customers.

## What looks crowded

Several positions have abundant supply:

- generic event discovery;
- generic ticket checkout;
- young urban singles mixers;
- “AI matches you with people” without a distinctive occasion;
- broad creator communities with no format-specific operating advantage;
- large-city lists that do not reveal event frequency or fill.

A new entrant can still operate in these spaces, but a polished calendar is not differentiation. The company must own either demand, supply workflow, a format, a cohort or an economic advantage.

## Where the whitespace remains

### Regional intellectual nightlife

Seed Talks demonstrates UK touring breadth, but many regional markets can still support narrower locally owned programmes: science, history, design, practical philosophy, civic questions or founder stories. The opening is not an empty market; it is the ability to build a recognisable local institution with repeat venues and a distinctive editorial voice.

### Activity-led friendship for later life stages

The 35–65 segment can be served through making, walking, games, talks and gentle sport rather than only dinner. The social promise can remain explicit, but the activity removes the feeling of attending a loneliness intervention.

### Cohorts between a one-off and a club

Six-week courses, four-session campaigns, seasonal leagues and twelve-month adventure programmes create repeated exposure without asking for an indefinite membership on day one. This is a strong commercial middle ground.

### Local hosts with real economics

Reading Rhythms, SweatPals and Pint of View expose a supply-side opportunity: give a credible local lead a format, acquisition, payments, operating support and a transparent share. Most platforms sell tools; few can prove a chapter-level livelihood.

### The relationship layer across activities

Discovery products know what was booked. Managed social products know who attended together. Few appear to combine consented reconnection, prior-overlap management and group continuity across talks, sport, craft and games. That is a possible later product, but only after real attendance creates the data.

## What not to infer from the atlas

Forty-four operators do not prove forty-four successful businesses. A long city list can contain thin activity. A sold-out event can lose money. A free app install can be inactive. A corporate product page can have no meaningful sales. An operator's attendee count may be registrations, tickets or cumulative people and may include repeats.

The atlas is most useful as a library of mechanisms. The founder's job is to select a coherent combination rather than imitate the company with the loudest scale claim.
