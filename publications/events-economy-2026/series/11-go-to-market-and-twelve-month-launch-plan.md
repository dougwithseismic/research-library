# Go-to-market and twelve-month launch plan

## The objective

The first year should prove one repeatable **programme cell**, not maximise cities, registrations, formats or press mentions.

The core question is:

> Can one interest-led public programme acquire an audience economically, convert some attendees into a smaller repeated format and create positive contribution without founder heroics?

## Weeks 1–2: choose where to buy evidence

Use the [city opportunity scorecard](./26-city-opportunity-scorecard-and-launch-cells.md) as a shortlist, not a launch order. If the founder has no stronger home-market advantage, compare Bristol, Birmingham and Manchester.

For each finalist obtain:

- two written venue options for the same 60–100-person public format;
- one smaller room or restaurant option for an eight-to-fourteen-person depth format;
- two plausible facilitator or host candidates and one backup route;
- a realistic weekday catchment and last-travel-home boundary;
- a current sample of competing events at the intended price and time;
- one public event proposition and one related depth proposition at real prices.

### Gate

Select one city only when the format can break even below 75% paid capacity under a written venue scenario and at least two delivery people can operate it. A spreadsheet rank cannot rescue a bad venue agreement or a founder with no local access.

## Weeks 2–4: customer reconstruction before inventory

Interview at least:

- 16 prospective attendees across new-to-area, remote-work, post-separation, retirement and existing-local segments;
- 4 recent purchasers of talks, classes, games, clubs or social sport;
- 6 venue operators covering quiet weekday and busy weekend capacity;
- 6 experienced hosts, speakers, facilitators or community organisers;
- 3 safeguarding, event-risk or customer-operations specialists.

Ask for recent behaviour, not agreement with the concept. “When did you last try to meet new people?” is useful. “Would you use an app that helps you meet people?” is not.

Reconstruct the last paid night out: discovery source, companion or solo decision, ticket price, travel, arrival anxiety, what happened in the room and whether the person returned. Show one concrete public event and one depth follow-up only after the reconstruction.

### Gate

Proceed only if at least twenty target participants provide a real availability window, twelve accept a stated public-event price and eight accept the smaller follow-up price or deposit. Interest without time, place and price is not demand.

## Months 2–3: deliver one six-event season

Run one deliberately narrow season using existing ticketing and customer tools:

- two 60–100-person public nights built around the same editorial or activity promise;
- one 30–40-person participatory reading, game or low-equipment making night;
- two eight-to-fourteen-person hosted dinners, salons or short-course sessions derived from the public programme;
- one repeat of the strongest format, not a sixth unrelated experiment.

At least two people other than the founder should deliver meaningful parts of the programme. Use manual assignment for small groups with documented reasons. Do not build membership, a marketplace or custom matching software.

For each event capture:

- inquiries, event-page views, checkout starts and paid bookings;
- full-price share and booking lead time;
- cancellations before and after the boundary;
- predicted and actual attendance;
- solo bookings and arrival-comfort feedback;
- host, facilitator and venue time;
- direct cost and contribution;
- severe-comfort, conduct and safety reports;
- public-to-depth invitation and booking;
- rebooking within 30 and 60 days;
- source and referral.

### Gate

Continue only if at least four of six events clear minimum viable size, aggregate show-up reaches 80%, every format has a legible direct-contribution result and at least 20% of first attendees make a second paid booking or deposit within sixty days.

## Months 4–5: repeat the same promise

Repeat the winning public format and one related depth format on the same weekday and venue pattern. Retire weak novelty. Recruit a second and backup host or facilitator. Establish a venue scorecard and negotiate a repeat operating agreement based on actual attendance, bar or food behaviour and operational burden.

Launch a simple referral:

```text
referrer receives credit only after the new guest attends
new guest receives a modest first-event credit
both rewards are capped and recorded separately from purchased credits
```

Do not reward registrations. The useful outcome is a new attended customer.

### Gate

Aim for at least 85% show-up, 25% first-to-second booking within sixty days and measurable public-to-depth conversion. These are management thresholds, not external benchmarks. If repeat is weak, interview attendees and non-returners before changing price, city or format.

## Months 6–7: productise operations

Build only the workflow manual operations have exposed:

- customer, consent and accessibility record;
- event capacity, break-even and cancellation thresholds;
- booking, pack and credit liability ledger;
- group assignment with review and reason codes;
- waitlist and reconfirmation;
- host and facilitator run sheet;
- incident, complaint and appeal route;
- public-to-depth invitation and conversion;
- venue, speaker and facilitator settlement;
- attendance and format-contribution reporting;
- data-subject request and deletion workflow.

Move operational truth out of personal spreadsheets and chat threads. Do not add public profiles, direct messaging or AI personality matching.

### Gate

Every booking, entitlement, assignment, attendance, settlement and safety action should be reconcilable. If the team cannot explain a group or cash balance, adding another event increases risk.

## Months 8–9: make return behaviour purchasable

Offer a bounded three-event season, four-session cohort or capped membership to the most active customers. A membership experiment might be £24–£32 monthly for up to two standard reservations, with early access and paid additional seats. The exact offer must follow observed format costs. Keep pay-as-you-go available so the test does not force conversion.

Measure:

- invitation-to-trial rate;
- attended seats per member or season;
- direct cost per member-month or season;
- contribution after fulfilment;
- one-, two- and three-month retention where applicable;
- whether members crowd out pay-as-you-go customers;
- unused entitlement and stated value;
- effect on cancellations and no-shows.

### Gate

Do not offer unlimited access unless the upper-utilisation cohort remains contribution-positive and inventory availability is consistently good. Do not keep membership if a bounded season creates better cash, attendance and customer understanding.

## Months 10–12: one commercial adjacency

Choose one adjacency based on the asset the first nine months actually created:

- a standard corporate version of the winning public format;
- a venue-funded repeat slot with measured incremental covers;
- a recording or short course where speaker rights already exist;
- a daytime or alcohol-light version for the same customer group;
- a neighbouring district inside the same operating radius.

Run it as a separate cohort P&L. Do not bundle weak events into membership to hide demand and do not accept bespoke work that cannot reuse the programme.

### Final gate

Expand only if the original cell has positive direct contribution, repeat attendance, public-to-depth conversion, host redundancy, venue redundancy and a credible incident record. Otherwise remain local, raise price, change the programme or stop. A second city belongs after the twelve-month gate, not inside the first year's rescue plan.

## Acquisition portfolio

### 1. Referrals

Social trust transfers between people, but referral volume is not automatically high quality. Reward attendance rather than signups, track referred contribution and prevent one promoter from distorting a cohort.

### 2. Venue and facilitator distribution

Use venue newsletters, table cards, staff recommendations, speaker lists and facilitator communities only where the partner can explain the product accurately. Give each partner a trackable but privacy-preserving source code. Share value after attendance, not after a click.

### 3. Local editorial

Local radio, newspapers, neighbourhood newsletters and founder stories fit a regional trust service. The pitch should be the actual subject, format and audience, not unsupported loneliness or wellbeing outcomes.

### 4. Format-led search

Build landing pages around the thing being bought—reading night, social games, pottery workshop, expert talk or hosted dinner—while explaining solo arrival and the relationship layer. Use paid search as a bounded experiment because activity phrases contain mixed intent and CPC-to-attended-customer economics remain unknown.

### 5. Partnerships

Test relocation providers, coworking spaces, alumni groups, libraries, bookshops, cultural venues and large employers. Keep institutional cohorts separate until members explicitly choose cross-cohort events.

### 6. Social content

Show the arrival, host, format and venue without exposing attendees who did not give specific media consent. Testimonials should identify when an attendee received free access or another benefit.

## The metric tree

```text
qualified local reach
→ event-page view
→ paid booking
→ reconfirmed booking
→ attended seat
→ comfortable event
→ depth invitation
→ second paid booking
→ referred attended guest
→ 180-day contribution
```

Supporting operational measures:

- event fill at 14 days, 7 days and 72 hours;
- full-price share;
- show-up and waitlist recovery;
- host, facilitator and venue cancellation;
- contribution per occupied seat and per format;
- direct support minutes per attended seat;
- credits and undelivered season liability;
- material incident and resolution rate;
- cohort concentration by format and acquisition source.

## Experiments worth running

| Question                            | Test                                                        | Primary measure                    | Decision                                                |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Does the activity earn acquisition? | Interest-led page versus friendship-led page                | Cost per attended first-timer      | Lead with the promise that produces paid attendance     |
| Does the depth layer add value?     | Public-only follow-up versus related small-group invitation | Second booking and 90-day value    | Keep depth only if it improves return or contribution   |
| Does hosting matter?                | Trained host versus light-touch coordinator                 | Arrival comfort and second booking | Keep the service level that produces incremental repeat |
| Does age guidance help?             | Broad adult group versus life-stage-guided group            | Fill, comfort and repeat           | Use the least restrictive segmentation that works       |
| Does commitment reduce no-shows?    | Refundable deposit versus paid reservation                  | Attended contribution              | Prefer the simplest effective commitment                |
| Is a season better than membership? | Three-event season versus capped monthly access             | Cash, usage, repeat and liability  | Sell the bounded promise that retains contribution      |
| Which slot is viable?               | Tuesday versus Thursday                                     | Fill and contribution              | Concentrate inventory rather than maintain weak choice  |
| Can venues acquire customers?       | Tracked venue referral                                      | Cost per attended first guest      | Share value only after attendance                       |

## What not to do in year one

- Launch ten cities from a waitlist map.
- Run three pilot cities because the desk scorecard contains three finalists.
- Promise algorithmic compatibility before observing group quality.
- Mix dating and friendship without separate intent and events.
- Build direct messaging before moderation capacity exists.
- Subsidise empty rooms to produce photos.
- Use free events as evidence for paid retention.
- Report restaurant spend as platform GMV without explaining the split.
- Sell brand access to sensitive member segments.
- Expand because a city achieved registrations rather than repeat attendance.

The first year's output is not a national brand. It is a verified programme cell, a direct audience and an honest answer about whether public interest can create repeated local relationships.
